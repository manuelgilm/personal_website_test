AI has become the default place to think out loud. Terms like ChatGPT or Claude show up in everyday conversations, and for a lot of people the model is now the first stop for advice — including personal decisions and half-formed ideas that used to end up in a browser tab.

Part of the appeal is speed. A few seconds gets you a long, well-argued answer that reads like the final word. That is also the trap: whichever provider you use, these models tend to sound extremely confident. Whatever comes out looks like a fact you would be silly to question.

They are not facts, though. They are probabilistic systems, and their answers come from patterns in training data that may be outdated, incomplete, or simply invented. Confident is not the same as correct, which is why an answer always deserves to be checked.

Now keep the idea exactly the same and add a system prompt. The system prompt is the fastest lever you have over a model's output. You are not changing parameters and you are not fine-tuning anything, yet the answer moves a lot.

## Positive feedback

Ask the model to be encouraging but honest and it will find the strengths: what is genuinely promising in the idea and where it has room to grow. Curiosity, not flattery, is what you want here. A warm first pass is useful because it tells you which parts are worth protecting before you start cutting.

<IdeaFeedback persona="optimist" label="Try it: strengths-first review" />

Compared to the plain answer above, the tone shifts on its own. Nothing about the idea changed — only the instruction the model was given. It is the same model, wearing a different hat.

## Critical feedback

Now flip the prompt to a skeptic. This pass hunts for the weak assumptions, the reasons the idea could fail, and the parts that only look good because you want them to. It is less pleasant to read, and that is the point: you are paying for the objections you would rather not hear.

<IdeaFeedback persona="skeptic" label="Try it: critical review" />

Two prompts, two very different verdicts on the same idea. Neither is the truth. Each is one perspective, and a single perspective is a single sample.

## How to fix it

Criticism without a next step is just noise. Point the model at what it would actually take to move forward — the concrete actions, the resources, and the first thing worth testing. This is the pass that turns objections into a plan.

<IdeaFeedback persona="pragmatist" label="Try it: how to fix it" />

You can see the difference: the same idea, read through a pragmatic lens, sounds more grounded and realistic than either the cheerleading or the takedown. That is not because the model got smarter. It is because the question changed.

## Overall

Each persona is a slice. To put the slices together, hand the answers to a synthesizer: a prompt that receives the idea and the feedback from the other perspectives, weighs where they agree and where they conflict, and returns an overall score from 1 to 10 with a reason.

<IdeaFeedback persona="synthesizer" label="Try it: overall view" />

The score is a summary, not a verdict. It compresses three opinions into one number, and it is built on top of the same model that produced them. Useful for orientation, not for certainty.

## Reading the confidence

Every reply ends with a line telling you how confident the model claims to be. Treat that as a signal, never as a probability. Models are frequently overconfident — the number they report and the chance they are actually right are two very different things.

Two habits help more than any single confidence score:

- **Look for agreement.** When the optimist, the skeptic, and the pragmatist all land on the same concern, that is worth taking seriously. When they scatter, you have found genuine uncertainty.
- **Verify the parts that matter.** Anything you would act on — a number, a claim, a "best practice" — is worth a second source. The model will not tell you when it is guessing.

Asking the same question from several angles beats trusting a single confident answer. That is the whole trick: use the model as a panel, and keep the judgment for yourself.
